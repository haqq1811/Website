Who I Am

I didn't set out to become a data analyst. I fell into it the way most people find the thing they're actually good at, by getting stuck on a question nobody around me could answer with any confidence, and realizing a spreadsheet could get me closer to the truth than a gut feeling could.

That first small hit of "oh, so that's why that's happening" never really wore off. Every project since has been some version of the same chase: a mess of numbers, a question worth asking, and the quiet satisfaction of watching a pattern surface that wasn't visible a few hours earlier.

I'm not chasing certainty. I'm chasing the next question worth asking.

What keeps me in this field isn't the tooling, even though I do care about doing that part well. It's the people on the other side of every dataset — the customer behind the churn number, the decision behind the budget line. Numbers are just the fastest way I've found to get to what's actually going on.

How I Think About Data

Somewhere along the way, "data-driven" started to mean "cold." I don't think it has to. Here's roughly how I actually approach it:

01

A lens, not a verdict

Numbers can tell you what happened, but they rarely explain why on their own. I treat every dataset as the start of a conversation — a way to see a situation more clearly, not a gavel to bring down on it.

02

Curiosity before conclusions

It's tempting to go looking for the answer you already expect to find. I try to stay a little suspicious of my own hypotheses and let the data surprise me — that's usually where the more interesting story is hiding.

03

Clarity over cleverness

A brilliant model nobody can act on isn't worth much. I'd rather hand someone a simple, honest chart they actually understand than a complex one that impresses for five seconds and then gets ignored.

Beyond The Data

Outside of work, I'm probably the same curious, slightly restless person I am at my desk — just pointed at different questions.

I care a lot about the small stuff: a good conversation, a well-made meal, the kind of quiet that lets you actually think something through. I try to bring the same patience I use with a messy dataset to the rest of my life — slow down, look closer, don't assume you already know the answer.
